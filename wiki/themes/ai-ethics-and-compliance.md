# AI ethics and compliance

Legal and regulatory pressure on AI hiring is accelerating, though federal enforcement is receding while state and international requirements tighten. Federal judges are holding AI vendors liable alongside employers for discrimination (Mobley v. Workday); the EU AI Act's high-risk deadline for hiring tools is **contested in this corpus** — a 3 July report has the Council giving final sign-off to a deferral from 2 August 2026 to 2 December 2027, while a 2 August report has those obligations becoming applicable on 2 August with the deferral still only proposed; both are in the timeline below, and anyone hiring into the EU should confirm the live date with counsel rather than rely on either; and a patchwork of state laws (Colorado, New York, California) now governs AI employment decisions. Meanwhile, applicant trust is collapsing (only 26% trust AI to evaluate them fairly) and AI-enabled discrimination is widening beyond hiring: Meta faces the first major lawsuit over AI in layoffs. A parallel risk is emerging as AI adoption becomes universal: candidate fraud and authenticity verification. 87% of hiring managers now use AI at some stage, but 86% expect AI to make verifying authenticity harder — with 58% already seeing AI-generated resumes and 17% catching deepfakes in video. Counter to the "AI is freezing hiring" narrative, SMBs using AI are roughly four times more likely to report hiring growth than cuts — suggesting the automation risk is mismatch (wrong skills hired faster) rather than net job elimination. The pattern is clear — "the algorithm did it" is no longer a defense, and compliance risk spans legal liability, bias auditing, documented human oversight, and identity verification. TA leaders must audit tools for bias, document human oversight at every consequential decision point, and build fraud detection into the funnel.
